A guide for parents of bilingual kids

You raised them in your language. Now they're starting school.

For a lot of families, the first years of school are when the home language starts to slip. This guide covers what the research says happens at that point, how to think about school options, and the small habits that help children keep both languages.

Part One

Basic linguistic terms

The ideas researchers use to describe bilingual families, and how they probably already show up in your home.

IChapter 1·Before school

The basics, in plain language

Researchers have names for things many bilingual families already do. Knowing them makes the advice later on easier to use.

Family language policy

Your family's plan for its languages

A family language policy is the deliberate choices a family makes about which languages get used at home, by whom, and for reading and writing. (King, Fogle & Logan-Terry, 2008)

Some families plan this carefully, like "Mom speaks only Spanish." Others keep it loose and flexible. Both count, and most families sit somewhere in between.

BFLA → SLA

When a second language starts matters

A child who hears two languages from birth is doing bilingual first language acquisition (BFLA). A child who starts a new language later, at school for example, is doing second language acquisition (SLA). (Ortega, 2013, p. 4)

The line between them blurs around ages 3 to 4, which is when many kids start preschool. That's why the start of school is such a big moment.

Complementarity Principle

Each language has its own jobs

Bilinguals usually use their languages for different purposes, in different parts of life, with different people. Very few use every language for everything. (Grosjean, 2010)

One boy in De Houwer's study knew the word "boots" only in English. That isn't a problem. It just shows where each language gets used. (De Houwer, 2009, p. 60)

The Planner

Your family already has a deliberate language policy, with set people, times or rules. That's a strong base. The main risk comes when school starts and the school language pushes into the times you set aside. Part Two shows what to watch for.

Go with the Flow

Your family uses both languages naturally, without many rules. Many families live this way, and it works while both languages are needed. Once school starts, the school language usually takes over the open moments, so it helps to set aside a few times when the home language is the default.

Drifting toward the school language

The school language is already doing most of the work at home. That's the most common pattern, not a failure, and it can still be turned around. Start with Part Two to see how it happens, then the habits in Part Three.

    IIIChapter 3·The first year

    How a home language slips away

    Researchers keep finding the same pattern in families. It usually happens gradually, and most of the time nobody chooses it. (De Houwer, 2009, p. 63; Wong Fillmore, 1991)

    1. School news comes home in the school language

      Your child has learned about the day in English, so they tell you about it in English.

      Step in: Ask follow-up questions in your language. Act interested in the story, and don't correct them.

    2. Parents stop switching back

      Answering in English is easier and keeps the conversation going, so you start doing it more often.

      Step in: Choose a few times of day, like dinner or bedtime, when the home language is the default.

    3. Each person speaks a different language

      You speak your language, your child answers in English, and that becomes the family habit.

      Step in: Create real reasons to speak it, like video calls with grandparents or friends who need the language.

    4. Your child understands but can't speak it

      Children can lose the ability to speak the home language. In Wong Fillmore's study, families lost the language they used to talk to each other.

      Step in: This can still be turned around. Passive understanding is a real foundation to build on.

    !

    Why it isn't your fault. For many families, raising a child bilingually was never really a free choice. It's simply what life looks like. (De Houwer, 2009, p. 68) Parents often get advice with no basis in research and little support, and the guilt and stress that follow are common. (Lanza, 2007, cited in De Houwer, 2009, p. 71) This guide is here to fill that gap.

    VIIChapter 7·Myths and misconceptions

    Myths and misconceptions

    Many families give up a home language because of advice that sounds reasonable but doesn't hold up. Decide whether each statement is true or false, then see what the research from our course readings says.

    Common myths vs. what research says

    Myth

    "One parent, one language" is the only method that works.

    What research says

    No single family strategy, including One-Parent-One-Language, reliably produces a child who actively speaks both languages. How much a child hears each language, and whether they need it, matter more. (Fogle & King, 2013; De Houwer, 2009, p. 70)

    Myth

    If my child can speak the language, reading and writing will follow.

    What research says

    In a study of Korean American families, everyday language habits predicted speaking skills, but keeping reading and writing took active effort from parents. (Kang, 2015, Bilingual Research Journal)

    Myth

    Once we choose a plan, we're set.

    What research says

    Even a well-defined family language policy can be thrown off by big disruptions and small life changes, so plans need updating over time. (Pittman & Glimois, 2024)

    Myth

    If my child loses fluency, it's too late.

    What research says

    Heritage speakers are a distinct kind of learner with partial knowledge of the family language, and many work to relearn or improve it later in life. (Montrul, 2010, Annual Review of Applied Linguistics)

    Myth

    Any dual language program will serve home-language families well.

    What research says

    When two-way immersion spreads without critical attention, it can treat Latino families' languages as a commodity instead of empowering those families. It's worth asking who a program is designed for. (Cervantes-Soon, 2014, Bilingual Research Journal)

    Myth

    Keeping a language is just a matter of willpower.

    What research says

    A heritage language is tied to the community that speaks it and to identity (He, 2010), and keeping it up carries real emotions, including guilt and pressure. (Song & Wu, 2024, Modern Language Journal)

    IVChapter 4·Choosing a school

    Bilingual schooling options and the transition point

    No school does all of the work. Each setting changes how much of the home language your family has to keep up on its own. Start with what's actually available near you.

    The transition point: what to do and when

    For many families the switch happens around ages 3 to 5, when preschool or kindergarten starts. That's also where bilingual first language acquisition starts to blur into second language acquisition. (Ortega, 2013, p. 4)

    1. Before enrolling

      Find out which programs near you teach in your home language, and ask each school how long that support lasts. Programs can be cut, so ask how stable they are. (De Houwer, 2009, pp. 61–62)

    2. The first weeks

      Expect school stories to come home in the school language. Keep your home-language routines, like dinner or bedtime, exactly as they were. (De Houwer, 2009, p. 63)

    3. The first year

      Check in every few months: Who is your child speaking the home language with? Is anyone still asking them to? Adjust your plan if the answer is "almost no one."

    4. Later on

      Add reading and writing in the home language, through a weekend class or books at home, so it keeps growing alongside school. (Ortega, 2013, p. 6)

    What the long-term research says

    The largest U.S. study of this question followed more than 210,000 student records. Right after leaving language support, children from all-English programs scored higher in English than children from bilingual programs. Over the long run, the pattern flipped: the more years of grade-level schooling a child had in their first language, the higher their English achievement. (Thomas & Collier, 2002) In the authors' long-term data, dual language programs were the ones that closed the gap with native English speakers, and most other programs did not. (Collier & Thomas, 2004)

    The catch: school support can disappear. In De Houwer's case study, one boy's Spanish program was cancelled, which left home to do the work. (De Houwer, 2009, pp. 61–62) So whatever school you choose, have a home plan too.

    VChapter 5·At home

    Six habits that protect the home language

    Once a child is learning the school language at school, the home language starts working more like a heritage language: one that's mostly used at home and in the community. (Ortega, 2013, p. 6) These habits come from the families researchers have studied.

    1

    Give them a real reason to use it

    One girl understood Korean for years but didn't speak it until a Korean classmate actually needed her help. Children speak a language when someone needs them to. (De Houwer, 2009, p. 62)

    This week: regular calls with a grandparent who doesn't speak English.

    2

    Count the hours

    How much a child hears a language matters. That same girl heard Korean only about five hours a week, while another child heard both languages every day. (De Houwer, 2009, p. 70)

    This week: write down roughly how many hours a week your child hears the home language.

    3

    Read together

    One mother in De Houwer's study read books with her son to make up for how little Korean he heard during the week. (De Houwer, 2009, p. 71)

    This week: one bedtime book a night in the home language.

    4

    Decide about reading and writing

    School teaches reading and writing. At home, a language is picked up through daily life. (Ortega, 2013, p. 6) Choosing which language to read and write in first is a real family decision. One family had their daughter learn to write in Dutch before Mandarin. (De Houwer, 2009, p. 59)

    This week: look into a weekend class for reading and writing in the home language.

    5

    Don't compare them to monolingual kids

    A bilingual child isn't two monolingual children in one person. Judging them against kids who speak only one language misses what they can actually do. (Ortega, 2013, pp. 5–6, 9; Cook, 1991)

    This week: notice what your child can do across both languages put together.

    6

    Expect effort, not a talent

    You don't need a "language person" for a child to stay bilingual. The hype around gifted language learners hides how much time and effort learning languages takes for everyone. (Pavlenko, 2015)

    This week: check in on your plan every few months, and change it when it isn't working.

    VIChapter 6·Working with teachers

    If a teacher suggests dropping your language

    In one family De Houwer describes, a school counselor said two languages were harming their son and pushed for special education. (De Houwer, 2009, p. 60) Advice like this is common, and it doesn't hold up against the research. These points can help you prepare for a parent–teacher meeting.

    Talking points for a parent–teacher meeting

    1. Share the plan. Explain which language is used at home, by whom, and why it matters to your family (for example, it's how your child talks with grandparents).
    2. Ask for specifics. Which English skills is your child working on? Concrete goals are easier to support at home than a general worry.
    3. Ask about both languages. A bilingual child's full ability shows across both languages, not in one alone. (Ortega, 2013, pp. 5–6)
    4. Get a second opinion if needed. If special education or speech services come up, ask for an evaluation by someone experienced with bilingual children.

    VIIIChapter 8·Outside of school

    Options that aren't school

    Whatever school your child attends, these options can carry the home language. Each one fills a gap the research points to.

    Weekend heritage schools

    Community schools, often on Saturdays, that teach reading, writing, and culture in the home language.

    Why it fits the research: Speaking can survive on everyday habits, but reading and writing take active effort (Kang, 2015). These schools add the instructed side of learning that home life doesn't usually provide. (Ortega, 2013, p. 6)

    Cultural centers and community groups

    Embassy cultural centers, places of worship, and community organizations that run classes and events in the language.

    Why it fits the research: A heritage language lives in the community that speaks it (He, 2010), and children use a language when the people around them need them to. (De Houwer, 2009, p. 62)

    Reading and writing at home

    Bedtime books, songs, letters or texts to relatives, and labels around the house in the home language.

    Why it fits the research: Parents in a TABE journal study connected children's literacy to the home environment (Compean-Garcia, 2013), and one mother in De Houwer's study used daily reading to make up for low input. (De Houwer, 2009, p. 71)

    Real reasons to use it

    Regular calls with relatives, trips, playdates with children who speak the language, and summer programs.

    Why it fits the research: Toshie only started speaking Korean when a classmate needed her to (De Houwer, 2009, p. 62). Each language survives in the parts of life where it has a job. (Grosjean, 2010)

    IXChapter 9·Close to home

    Examples in the Washington, D.C. area

    Real programs near D.C., grouped by type, with the research that explains why each type can help. Programs change, so check each one's current offerings and application process.

    Public · D.C.

    DC Public Schools dual language

    Spanish–English dual language programs at schools including Oyster-Adams, Bancroft, Bruce-Monroe, Marie Reed, Powell, Columbia Heights EC, and Roosevelt HS. Oyster-Adams has offered bilingual instruction for more than 50 years.

    Why it fits the research: Over the long run, students with more years of schooling in their first language did better in English, and dual language programs were the ones that closed achievement gaps. (Thomas & Collier, 2002; Collier & Thomas, 2004)

    DCPS dual language programs
    Public charter · D.C.

    Charter immersion schools

    Mundo Verde, DC Bilingual, and LAMB (Spanish); Washington Yu Ying (Mandarin); Elsie Whitlow Stokes (Spanish and French); and Sela (Hebrew). DC International School continues immersion in Chinese, French, or Spanish for grades 6–12.

    Why it fits the research: A program that continues into middle and high school matters, because support that stops early leaves home to carry the load (De Houwer, 2009, pp. 61–62). Parents choosing two-way programs often expect more opportunities and stronger communication skills (López, 2013); it's also worth asking who a program is built to serve. (Cervantes-Soon, 2014)

    D.C. Policy Center on dual language demand
    Public · Northern Virginia

    Fairfax County dual language immersion

    Half-day immersion in French, German, Japanese, Korean, or Spanish, through a countywide lottery for rising kindergarteners and first graders. Colin Powell Elementary in Centreville runs Virginia's only Korean immersion program.

    Why it fits the research: Starting at age 5 or 6 lands right at the point where bilingual first language acquisition blurs into second language acquisition (Ortega, 2013, p. 4). For a Korean-speaking family, it gives the home language a job during the school day and builds the literacy that families struggle to keep up alone. (Kang, 2015)

    FCPS dual language immersion
    Weekend and community · D.C. area

    Heritage language schools and centers

    Saturday Korean schools such as the United Korean School of Washington; Korean classes through the Korean Cultural Center and the Korean School Institute of DC; Chinese schools such as Wei Hwa Chinese School and the Washington School of Chinese Language and Culture; and the Washington Japanese Language School.

    Why it fits the research: These fill the gap when a family's language isn't offered at school, adding reading and writing and a community that needs the language. (Kang, 2015; He, 2010)

    Korean Cultural Center classes

    Demand is high in D.C.: in 2023–24, the average waitlist for PK3 dual language and immersion programs was 126 students, compared with 35 for other schools. Apply early and have a backup plan. This list is a starting point, not an endorsement.

    Growing up multilingual is normal

    Most people in the world grow up with more than one language. (Ortega, 2013, p. 4; Pavlenko, 2015) Your family doesn't need a perfect method. It needs a plan you come back to, especially when school starts.
